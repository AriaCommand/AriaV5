Under the harsh glare of the spotlight, Patchwork Mary stepped on stage. The hushed whispers of the audience ebbed and flowed into an eerie hush as the spotlight beamed down exposing piercing green eyes and a bright toothy smile on a face webbed with scars. Her hands remained bound behind her back. Next to her was a ladder leading at least two stories to a small platform, and below it, a large tank of water that she calibrated to self lock upon receiving the exact weight threshold of herself and the water.
Patchwork Mary smiled and waited just long enough until the audience can get their applause in. They all paid for the show, so it wasn't like the promise of her performance wasn't guaranteed. Yet, she played the game of humbly bowing and smiling, while her leg grew numb from the new weight from her latest contraption hidden within her performance outfit. The audience saw art. Mary felt braces that would make her performance perfect.
And it was perfect.
In a single jump, Mary ignored the ladder entirely and leaped onto the second story platform. She landed in silence, then sound. Always silence first, then the roar. A brace in her leg screamed. She winced, but quickly bit her tongue and revealed another toothy grin. The audience screamed louder. Blood in the boot, bleeding tongue, but perfect form —that was the bargain. One more scar for one more roar. She'd patch it up later. With that, she free fall backwards into the tank. Everything in the world disappeared in that moment. She heard nothing as she fell. Then water. The light of the ceiling above her fading into a watery hue. The tank closed as planned as she sank deeper and deeper into the water.
That's when she saw it. A screw. A loose screw had escaped a joint when she hit the water. To what that screw went to? Mary had no idea. The irony that her life could end on a loose screw was beyond her, but she knew she'd troubleshoot this and still escape. Because Patchwork Mary never missed.
But then again, she hadn't always been Patchwork. Before the brace. Before possibly drowning on stage. Before she knew what 'sacrifice' really meant. She was Mary. Mary's tale is told in a number of ways that don't quite grasp the big picture. The retellings and accounts serves as patches at best in weaving the peculiar legend of Patchwork Mary. Though, if one were to trace all these threads, they would find Patchwork Mary's tale begins and ends with a Tin Man named Rufus Batchet.
Rufus Batchet lived in the Wasteland. The Wasteland existed just outside the city of Aurea. It was a dusty barren land littered with the remains of failed Tin Men welded together that formed the very foundation of the buildings where the inhabitants grew to become almost as grotesque as the buildings they inhabit. It was no place for the successful and famous. That right belonged to Aurea: The City of Stars. No, the Wasteland was where the city dumped its waste a failures who couldn't make it in Aurea —people and items included.
Rufus Batchet knew one day he too would become part of the many fallen Tin Men before him. Lack of sleep and coffee could only take a man so far, while organs can only be replaced by machinery for so long.
"Coffee. Triple espresso," a large man in a tank top that had long decided to remain a dusty yellow said as he laid down a chipped mug in front of Rufus.
Rufus gave the man nod.
"Thank you," Rufus said. To which, the man only grunted and returned to the rest of the orders he had. Rufus's face reflected back at him from within the dark contents of his drink. He'd grown old. Practically a shadow of his former self. A few streaks of grey had cropped up amongst his shaggy brown hair, while some salt and pepper marred his face. He closed his eyes as he enjoyed a long sip of the coffee as if it were the finest in all of the continent. Taste had become secondary to the rejuvenating feeling of caffeine hitting every fiber of his being.
How this became one of the highlights of Rufus's day? Life truly was strange. Rufus sighed and almost laughed at life's sick sense of humor as he looked around the dingy diner he sat in. However, he knew exactly the roads that led him to this moment. He reached into his pocket and gave a squeeze to the gem in his pocket. It was both a comforting action and one that gave his pause on what his current research was aiming to accomplish. Whatever revery or second guessing Rufus had, was however interrupted by a man who slumped down directly to his left. He was not quite as large as the man behind the counter cooking and serving drinks, but still large man in stained overalls. But unlike the cook, this man wore large thick gloves as were the hallmark of rummagers.
"You're the doctor?" The man grunted.
Rufus blinked. Why people still called him such was beyond him, but it didn't stop him from feel the tinge of pain for his former life every time he heard it.
"Yes, I'm going to take a wager you're the rummager I requested. Mister…" Rufus said. He laid down his cracked mug. He gave a sigh of hesitation and unsureness. "Ikabar Bobolar Boze?"
"You really are a learned man, doctor," Ikabar said. "Not many get it right on the first go. You can call me BoBo. All my friends do. "
"I think I'll go with Ikabar," Rufus said. "Do you have what I asked for?"
Ikabar gave a nod and kicked at the large burlap sack that sat behind them at his feet.
"Not exactly an easy grocery list, doctor," Ikabar said. "Lot of trash to rummage through, but your man BoBo here has got you." Rufus only nodded as he took another long sip of his coffee. Ikabar, on the other hand, just looked Rufus up and down asking the one question everyone asked in the Wasteland. "So how'd you end up in the Wasteland?"
Rufus winced at the question.
"Life just didn't go my way," Rufus said with a smile. It was a practiced smile that told as much as he was comfortable with sharing. Once upon a time a woman taught him how valuable a smile like that was at his disposal.
"Ah, so a woman," Ikabar said with a knowing nod.
Rufus gave a nervous laugh.
"Your daughter," Rufus said abruptly. "Give her this twice a day. Once in the morning and once at night and her fever should subside in a few days. If she sweats, make sure to give her a change of clothes or just wrap her in a warm blanket as the clothes she wore dry. Make sure she doesn't stay cold. I also wrote the instructions on the paper wrapped around the veil."
Ikabar took the little container with the droplet at the cap. It was one of the last pieces of medical equipment Rufus had, but it was a worthy trade for what he was working on. His sole reason for becoming a Tin Man.
"Thank you, doctor," Ikabar said."I'll be sure to stop by the workshop to thank you. They told me you were scary, but mainly they told me you have a soft spot for helping kids. I am in your debt. You a father as well?"
"Once," Rufus said quietly.
"Well," Ikabar said as he pointed a large sausage finger at Rufus's chest and narrowed his eyes. "You. You are now a friend of BoBo's. If you need rummaging, just call good ole BoBo."
"Will do," Rufus said. He finished up his coffee and nodded at the large man behind the counter that he was leaving payment before turning his attention to Ikabar. He reached for the burlap sack. Rufus gave a groan feeling it was heavier than he'd expected, but he was still able to lift it. "Now! I must be on my way. I have to finish what I started."
Rufus gave a coarse cough as he stepped outside into the Wasteland. Dust clouds shot by with the latest breeze. He pulled up a bandana over his mouth as he struggled to carry the burlap sack back home. Rust town wasn't the biggest place, but it was now his home. Rufus watched children cough and kick bags of trash around, while adults sat listlessly with a far out look. Some just cried all day. Crying was a normal occurrence, especially to the new residents of the Wasteland.
Rufus simply shrugged it off and made his way to the workshop. It wasn't the prettiest, but it was home to the remnant of his former life and the life he had now. A mesh of almost expired medical equipment and drugs that he manage to bring with him and scrap metal and cracked blackboards and tables he managed to find that served as his desk. Though, at the back of the workshop was a dirty decommissioned operating table with large rag draped over what he called his life's work.
Rufus gave a sigh and began to lay down the burlap sack at the foot of the operating table. The bag dropped with a thud with the opening revealing severed body parts. If there was a time to stop and give up what he was doing, it was then. However, he then looked over at a faded photo he kept from his former life. It was of him, clean cut, bright eyed in a doctor's coat with a young woman with bright green eyes next to him smiling brightly for the photo.
"Oh life you fickle bitch," Rufus said. He shook his head. "I guess it's showtime."
He sat down at the stool and began taking limbs and measuring them. Some would just get tossed into a bucket, while others he'd give a nod and start stitching the limb to the thing on the operating table. The operating light overhead would occasionally flicker and the job was grusome enough to cause Rufus to cringe and flinch at times as his consciousness reared its head into his mind at how crazy this all was. But he'd done the calculations. It was doable. The science was sound.
Stitch after stitch. Limbs upon limbs that were meticulously measured and sized before Rufus deemed usable. Then came the machinery of it all. Rufus wore a face mask as he started wrenching and soddering bits of the chest. Sparks occasionally singe the limbs that lay on the table. The head remained covered, but that he knew wasn't done yet.
The chest cavity he called the pace maker. It was what made the whole project possible. He knew every bit of teach he had said that this was insane and highly unethical, but he continued to work. He then reached for a jar of green eyes that ebbed and flowed in the liquid. Rufus disinfectanted tongs before applying the eyes into their sockets.
Then came the moment. Rufus reached inside his pocket again and pulled out the ruby gem.
"Mother, I know you wouldn't approve," Rufus said quietly. "But I need your help one last time."
He looked at the ruby gem before slowly dropping it into the chest cavity of the pace maker. He then took his sutures and began sealing up the chest. He lay a sheet over the body on the operating table and then grabbed an old defibrillator. He started the charge. Almost out of habit he said, "clear" with each pulse he gave the body.
Three charges before the a pair of green eyes flickered open.
